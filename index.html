Improving the prediction of high-risk transport refusals

A field checklist derived from linked prehospital records

Yachen Li

Supervised by Dr. David Buckeridge (McGill University) and Dr. Vianney Jouhet (Université de Bordeaux)

Background

Transport refusal

After a 9-1-1 call, a patient with decision-making capacity may decline the transport the crew recommends.

5–10%

of 9-1-1 calls in North American systems end with the patient refusing transport

6.3%

of non-conveyed patients were admitted to hospital within 72 hours, and 0.3% died

What happens on scene

Step 01

The patient refuses. The crew confirms capacity and explains the risks.

Step 02 · Refus à risque

The crew checks the refusal against 19 high-risk criteria

Any criterion present

Mandatory phone call to paramedic support from the scene

No criterion

No call

Step 03 · Every refusal

Recorded on audio and signed. The patient stays home.

A valid refusal must be respected. The task is to recognize the minority of refusals likely to end badly.

Methods

Model evaluation

Every measure is reported on the test set: June to December 2022, 9,920 refusals.

Operating point

Thresholds matched to the protocol

Each model's threshold was set on the validation set to reach the protocol's own sensitivity, then 85% and 90%. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV were then measured on the test set.

Discrimination

Does the rule rank risk correctly?

AUROC, with the area under the precision–recall curve as a check because only 6.5% of refusals had the outcome.

Calibration

Are the predicted risks the right size?

Calibration curve, slope and intercept, and Brier score.

Clinical utility

Are decisions better with the rule?

Decision curve analysis: net benefit compared with the current protocol, treating everyone and treating no one.

Discussion

Limitations inherent to the question

What the data can and cannot see

All 9-1-1 calls
Not seenTransported
Initially declined transport
Not seenAccepted transport after discussion
Final refusal
Not seen · 46%Not linked to both sources
Studied · 54%Linked: 94,825 refusals

Refusers are a selected group. Danger signs that usually lead to transport are rare among them, and some carry negative weights. Neither means the sign is safe.

Patients who were dissuaded appear as transports. The protocol was judged only on the refusals it did not prevent, so its true performance may be better than reported.

Half of the refusals could not be linked. The patients we could not see may be the ones at higher risk.

Discussion

Limitations of the data and methods

Data

The criteria the crew actually applied were not recorded; we rebuilt each one from the variables in the dataset.

Dispatch codes say what the complaint was, not how serious it was.

Split

Calls were split by date, so the same patient can appear in both the training and the test set.

The thresholds were chosen on a validation set with few events, so the sensitivity they give on new calls may differ from the target.

Model

The checklist was tested with its fitted weights, not as the simple form a crew would use.

Generalization

All data come from one EMS operator; the models were not validated elsewhere or across patient subgroups.
